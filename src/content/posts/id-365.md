You sit down to work at 9am, and two hours later are on Twitter wondering where your time went. What were you doing?

"Oh yeah, I was working on the UX for our landing page. Now - where was that email for Steve? And how did I end up on Twitter?"

Two hours later, you ask the same question again.

You went to your email but then you started processing your emails. You notice a JIRA comment, are about to respond, but need to find a recent Pull Request for context. You look for the PR, but then start reviewing open Pull Requests, and to find an obscure piece of knowledge, you Google the answer, find an excellent article you can't stop reading, and *have to share it* so now you're on Twitter, and oh look at these notifications.

Why didn't you stop yourself?!

Because most of that behavior was entirely unconscious.

Our brains turn off when we're in a well-rehearsed habit. This is great when the habit serves us. For example, driving would be extremely stressful if most of it didn't become automatic at some point. Do you remember your first time behind the wheel, and how much thinking was involved?

Interestingly, you don't even have to remember what you're doing to form new habits. You just have to do it enough that it becomes automatic.

In *The Power of Habit*, a patient known as Eugene is able to learn a wide array of useful behaviors in a new house. The twist is *he has complete and total amnesia.* But somehow he's able to learn. When asked where the kitchen is, he doesn't know. Or how to get to the bathroom. *But he does it anyway*.

Habits aren't learned like facts. They are *rehearsed*, until they can be done without thinking.

### Organizational Habits

I remember working on a team where one coworker was always getting (fortunately mostly affectionately) teased by some of the other team members. How this started isn't clear, but it spread. Soon, it became a norm for others.

At some point it was called out, and people stopped themselves and realized that they weren't behaving professionally. But before that, it just happened and spread without any conscious thought. It was rehearsed unquestioningly, and picked up like a disease.

Routines of behavior between people are a lot like routines we pick up on our own. Sometimes they form well and are useful to us. But often, behaviors form haphazardly based on things like how we're feeling the first few times something happens.

If you speak up in a meeting for the first time, and the boss comes down hard on your idea, what are the chances you're going to try again? After a few times, you get in the habit of seeing your boss as someone you can't talk to.

### Contagious Behaviors; Contagious Perceptions

Humans imitate behaviors. That's not surprising.

But the perceptions themselves that come from group behavior is also contagious.

In [a famous experiment](https://practicalpie.com/asch-line-study/), Solomon Asch subjected male college students to a vision test. The test was simple. Each student would say which of 3 lines on the right matched a line on the left. And the answer was obvious. The only catch was that before the experimental subject gave an answer, 11 people gave answers before him that were wrong.

In most cases, the student would give the answer that the other 11 gave.

What's striking about this is that while some of the participants lied (they wanted to fit in) and others simply doubted their judgement and assumed they saw wrongly, **one group actually started perceiving the situation differently** based on the answers of the preceding 11 actors.

While we might scoff at those people as being particularly weak-willed or feeble-minded, the scoffing would be misplaced.

Social creatures, such as humans, are constantly checking our perceptions against the perceptions of others. This is in part how we make sure we're sane, and it is also how insanity can rapidly spread through a group that spends a lot of time together. Like a company.

## Cultures as Creatures of Habit

Cultures at companies largely exist as habits between groups.

**Culture is largely a way of seeing, and every way of seeing correlates with performance.**

We see things without ever being aware of our eyes.

Similarly, we see without being aware of the perceptual filters we have on that are created by our goals, desires, and our theories about how the world is.

And again, we are unaware of our habitual ways of being and doing that give rise to yet another kind of filtering and distortion, which is caused by our habitual ways of feeling and thinking that are largely unconscious.

"There are no organizations without institutional habits. There are only places where they are deliberately designed, and places where they are created without forethought, so they often grow from rivalries or fear" - The Power of Habit.

If all cultures have habits, and habits affect our ways of seeing, how then do we change what we aren't aware of?

### Surfacing the unsaid and unaware

The process of habit change is largely the same in companies as it is in individuals.

It is a process of **becoming aware** of the thoughts that underlie our perceptions and behaviors, that are implicit in them, and are often currently no longer stated and often we cannot see.

**Surfacing thoughts that we mistake as in the objects themselves** **is the first step towards lasting change.**

"Steve never listens. He's great, but he never listens."

Can you change the way you behave towards Steve?

You can try opening up conversations, committing to giving Steve more information.

But strangely, you keep getting the same result: Steve isn't listening.

How do I get Steve to listen to me?

Is it true that Steve never listens? No, probably not.

How do I behave when that is how Steve occurs to me? Do I give Steve the real information? Give him the hard feedback?

Do I listen to Steve?

Until we separate out what is Steve and what is my judgement of Steve, our options for what we do in relation to Steve are constrained.

And so our options for what new response we might try to install in the habit loop are also constrained, and every response we choose in our new habit is going to be trying to deal with "Steve doesn't listen".

And we are likely to see the same results, because fundamentally, we go into each interaction with Steve with the same perception of Steve, and our actions are part of the interaction, and so we live into the way it occurs to us.

**Habit change is stickier when you change your identity, or your fundamental way of seeing and listening.**

To change habits, you have to change your response to the trigger, which means the trigger itself and the reward need to be understood.

Dealing with your world in this way is deep work, and personal work, and can be deeply unflattering.

"When Steve doesn't listen, I feel good about myself as someone who listens. I'm good, Steve is bad. I compare favorably to Steve."

If you had a different story, maybe you would slow Steve down, and try to fix the misunderstanding. But if "Steve doesn't listen", then you get a payoff without all that hard work of genuine communication.

Even admitting that is difficult. It doesn't feel good.

In order for a stimulus to lose its power, it must no longer have the same associations it used to have.

And for that to happen, we must through conversations surface the things that "go without saying", and say them, and ask if we intend to keep saying them implicitly with our actions.

It's a humbling process for everyone involved.

And it's not free. We all have to lay down our weapons we use to protect our egos, because we are committing to do something together.

**Only when the results we want together become more important to us than the results we get by continuing to hold our judgements can teams truly transform.**

## Keystone Habits as a driver for change

What is a Keystone Habit? Duhigg points out that keystone habits are "ones that, when they start to shift, dislodge and remake other patterns." Changing the organization "...doesn't depend on getting every single thing right, but instead relies on identifying a few key priorities and fashioning them into powerful levers."

In the book, he uses the example of Alcoa, which used the focus of safety to create new habits of excellence in the organization. Under his leadership, the company completely transformed.

"Within a year of O’Neill’s speech, Alcoa’s profits would hit a record high. By the time O’Neill retired in 2000, the company’s annual net income was five times larger than before he arrived, and its market capitalization had risen by \$27 billion."

But how did this work?

“I knew I had to transform Alcoa,” O’Neill told me. “But you can’t order people to change. That’s not how the brain works. So I decided I was going to start by focusing on one thing. If I could start disrupting the habits around one thing, it would spread throughout the entire company.”

Duhigg later goes on to elaborate: "Keystone habits say that success doesn’t depend on getting every single thing right, but instead relies on identifying a few key priorities and fashioning them into powerful levers."

Why does this work?

"What most people didn’t realize, however, was that O’Neill’s plan for getting to zero injuries entailed the most radical realignment in Alcoa’s history. The key to protecting Alcoa employees, O’Neill believed, was understanding why injuries happened in the first place. And to understand why injuries happened, you had to study how the manufacturing process was going wrong. To understand how things were going wrong, you had to bring in people who could educate workers about quality control and the most efficient work processes, so that it would be easier to do everything right, since correct work is also safer work."

He chose a focus on a system-wide goal that did several things simultaneously.

First, it was impossible to argue against from a union perspective, employee perspective, or management perspective. Everybody wanted safety.

Second, it created a new habit loop. When safety didn't happen (i.e. there was an accident), a new routine was put in place to identify the problem, and correct it.

Third, because it was on a system-wide goal, it **required radical shifts in communication*,*** which necessitates cultural shifts.

Finally, it created a new future that everyone could work towards. Alcoa was going to be the safest place.

All of the automatic routines that were not working well *had to be surfaced*, because any of them that contradicted safety would eventually come up, and it turns out that doing things correctly is also the safest way to do things.

His chosen focus both created a new future, and helped to raise the dysfunctions that were often hiding in plain sight.

It catalyzed the interactions and discussions that needed to happen in order to get to the roots of the problems.

"He instituted an automatic routine: Any time someone was injured, the unit president had to report it to O’Neill within twenty-four hours and present a plan for making sure the injury never happened again. And there was a reward: The only people who got promoted were those who embraced the system."

"Unit presidents were busy people. To contact O’Neill within twenty-four hours of an injury, they needed to hear about an accident from their vice presidents as soon as it happened. So vice presidents needed to be in constant communication with floor managers. And floor managers needed to get workers to raise warnings as soon as they saw a problem and keep a list of suggestions nearby, so that when the vice president asked for a plan, there was an idea box already full of possibilities. To make all of that happen, each unit had to build new communication systems that made it easier for the lowliest worker to get an idea to the loftiest executive, as fast as possible. Almost everything about the company’s rigid hierarchy had to change to accommodate O’Neill’s safety program."

This system wide focus, along with a policy change and an incentive forced everyone to work together towards a shared goal, which changed the culture, and was aligned with doing things well.

It also created a continuous improvement feedback loop because all issues that surfaced had to be solved, or at least attempted to be solved.

Tight feedback loops and open communication channels will change company performance.

To argue a bit with Duhigg, the story isn't about "small wins", but rather about writing a new future.

Safety was a system-wide concern of the company. It's hard to call it a keystone habit like he calls exercise, or other small changes.

In fact, it's almost the opposite of a "small win". Safety requires everyone in the company to operate differently.

But it's also something that forced everyone to wake up, and confront the true costs of what they were doing. I can no longer pretend that cutting corners is worth the cost of a potential accident, and that means I stop cutting corners.

When your organization gets in the habit of excellence, it changes how people feel about the organization, and it changes how people feel about themselves.

People start to see themselves as holding a high standard, part of a group that holds a high standard, and are ready and willing to do what it takes to do things right.

Conversely, when corner-cutting is encouraged, people can start to see themselves as part of a group that shirks some of its responsibilities. As needing to cover over weaknesses and deficiencies. And people who think they need to hide don't communicate as well. The culture shifts away from growth and accountability towards pretending and hiding.

Changing the way people occur to themselves and the way their organization occurs to them will dramatically shift performance.

The genius of focusing on safety is that it's a system-wide focus that also shifts the relationship of workers to the company. It builds reciprocity, and it builds good will, and it also requires examining everything that's not working, which helps rebuild every dysfunction into a strength.

The truth is that doing things well and doing things poorly is about the same level of effort. The only hard part is shifting your habit of doing and being from doing it poorly to doing it well. The big lift is usually during the transition. After that, things tend to keep working, and maybe continue improving if you put your attention on the right things.

Quote about when things really changed - it's when people started to see it as a serious thing, not just a passing fad. It began to occur to them as a serious shift, something that management would stick with.

“We killed this man,” a grim-faced O’Neill told the group. “It’s my failure of leadership. I caused his death. And it’s the failure of all of you in the chain of command.

The executives in the room were taken aback. Sure, a tragic accident had occurred, but tragic accidents were part of life at Alcoa. It was a huge company with employees who handled red-hot metal and dangerous machines. “Paul had come in as an outsider, and there was a lot of skepticism when he talked about safety,” said Bill O’Rourke, a top executive. “We figured it would last a few weeks, and then he would start focusing on something else. But that meeting really shook everyone up. He was serious about this stuff, serious enough that he would stay up nights worrying about some employee he’d never met.\

That’s when things started to change.” Within a week of that meeting, all the safety railings at Alcoa’s plants were repainted bright yellow, and new policies were written up. Managers told employees not to be afraid to suggest proactive maintenance, and rules were clarified so that no one would attempt unsafe repairs. The newfound vigilance resulted in a short-term, noticeable decline in the injury rate. Alcoa experienced a small win.\
Then O’Neill pounced. Workers made copies of the note and taped it to their lockers. Someone painted a mural of O’Neill on one of the walls of a smelting plant with a quote from the memo inscribed underneath. Just as Michael Phelps’s routines had nothing to do with swimming and everything to do with his success, so O’Neill’s efforts began snowballing into changes that were unrelated to safety, but transformative nonetheless.\
