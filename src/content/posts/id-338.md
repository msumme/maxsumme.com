### Local versus System Performance

If you are trying to figure out why your car won't start, you would have to look at a number of different parts.

Knowing that your car won't start isn't immediately actionable.

Systems are like this as well. If the system is functioning poorly, that matters. But it doesn't tell you what to do.

So how do we create the appropriate systems of measurement and monitoring at each level of the system to figure out the impacts of particular actions?

How do we create feedback loops that give the teams what they need at each level of the organization to take ownership of their own effectiveness?

And how do we look at the overall effectiveness of the system to make sure that the whole system is operating well

Add some other executives to the mix, wondering why their special new highest priority (whether or not it's easy, hard, or impossibly complex) isn't getting delivered according to their (possibly uninformed) expectations about reality, and you have a recipe for constant unnecessary change in how things are happening in your software development organization, and absolutely no answer to the question "Is this making things better?" until you get the very far away trailing indicators that the business is not meeting customer needs or shipping on time, or being competitive in the market.

So - if we all see this, why is it that we all think "Yes, those poor fools, but I actually know what works. Look at me." and then we measure success based on our own idea of what's working?

The first reason is simply the *Reality Illusion*, which means that people confuse their perceptions of what is with what is, and then cannot understand how anybody could think differently about it. We all perceive the same sets of actual events in different ways with different perspectives, and thus the situations occur to us so differently that it's like we're not even looking at the same movie.

The second reason is that even if we're all agreed on what the facts look like, we're not necessarily agreed on what the actual metrics of success are. This has more to do with local optimization versus systems-level thinking. What might look like success or an optimzation if you only consider a single team or a single piece of an organization actually could be the source of the higher-level dysfunction and failure of the organization as a whole.

If we zoom back to the level of the company as a system, and we can understand the inputs and outputs of that system, then we would be able, in theory, to detect when things are working or not working. (Aside, if you look at broader and broader systems, you can eventually see that optimizations for each individual part can damage the rest. One must see everything to make perfect decisions, which of course explains why large fully centralized planning usually fails, or at minimum dramatically underperforms more adaptive forms of organization).

But of course, now we are back to trailing indicators, which is often too slow of a feedback loop to be useful in adjusting what is being done at any given point in time.

So how do we rigorously figure out the indicators that would indicate the success of software engineering as software engineering, but also within the context of the organization? (i.e. How do we know if the engineering is working to provide the outputs that supports the remainder of the organization?)

Going back to our previous articles about what indicates success for software organizations, we can look again at the DORA metrics as a baseline for the speed of delivery, and the quality of delivery.

But how do we know that we're not only building quickly and well but that we are building ***what is effective***?

Are there actually common denominators for *effectiveness* of what's being built?

### How Planning Connects to Effectiveness

Planning a software roadmap generally has to connect to the rest of the business, and requires a high level of engagement with the customers and/or users of the software. Knowing what to build has to be front and center.

There are two ways to judge the way that things are going once we start building. 1) Are we executing on our plan, or are things getting delayed frequently for unplanned work (i.e. it isn't that the plan changed, things just happened like incidents or external events)? 2) What's the impact downstream?

Looking at the plan and how we're doing according to plan is the easiest way of determining if the engineering organization is performing, because the planning itself is only partially the responsibility of the engineering organization. If the rest of the business is giving bad information, and agreeing to a bad plan, then the most well-meaning and competent engineers aren't going to be able to solve the right problems.

Looking at downstream impact is a lot harder, and of course, we're back to trailing indicators again, which are useful but can't help us make adjustments at the right time.

Nonetheless, to have a full picture of how well the software engineering organization is working, we need to have a way of assessing how well what they're building is impacting the success of the organization, which is where we will need some KPIs for whatever company we're looking at. And we will need baseline measurements for sales and marketing and some way of attributing increased performance to product-market fit versus better sales/marketing (although can you separate these? Better marketing in the sense of finding a better-served group of customers for the product is also a way to improve your performance.)\

The complexity here can be staggering, which I think is why it's more resourceful to look at Plan Execution as the most important metric that solely pertains to the Engineering performance, and to look at Impact as something that the whole organization is responsible for (because your whole organization ought to be involved in negotiating priorities so that we're doing systems-level thinking instead of local optimizations).

If we pick Plan Execution as the main metric, we must now split Plan Disruptions into 3 categories: 1) Caused by Engineering itself, 2) Caused by broader organizational dysfunctions, 3) Caused by Environment (outside organization).

If a problem is caused from outside, we need to note it, and then decide if it falls into either 1) We could have accounted for this at our current level of awareness or 2) This was not predictable or 3) We could have accounted for this with increased awareness (and now we evaluate that cost).

The latter two scenarios don't matter in terms of engineering performance, and the first falls into a category of shared accountability (i.e. it's an organizational prioritization decision) and then analysis needs to happen to decide if we care (maybe it's better to take the chance that it would happen rather than spend resources, and that's what we did).

Looking at Plan Disruptions caused by Engineering and the Broader Organization gives us a certain level of confidence that engineering is either working or not working well, and that the broder organization is or is not dyfunctional in terms of understanding what it needs to do and creating plans that build in the right level of adaptability (i.e. plans can change, but there aren't lots of crises that disrupt development).

If you combine "Are we getting the plan done as predicted?" with broader "DORA" metrics, then you end up being able to group the engineering performance along with your peers and also see that it's likely serving the aims of the organization.

If you track impact in the marketplace over time, you can correlate your predictive measures with your actual outcomes.

Once the small feedback loops and meta feedback loops are in place, you can iteratively improve your techniques over time and better refine your model.
